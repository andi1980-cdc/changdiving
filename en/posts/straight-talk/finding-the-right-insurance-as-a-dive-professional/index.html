Finding the Right Insurance as a Dive Professional in Thailand

Health insurance, accident cover, diving exclusions and DAN diving insurance – what I learned after actually going through medical underwriting with several insurers in Thailand.

On this page
Why? My Situation Underwriting Comparison Diving Accident DAN OPD Broker Questions Shortlist Summary

When I first started looking for private health insurance in Thailand, I honestly thought it would take a few days.

It took several months.

During that time I contacted multiple insurance companies and brokers, compared policies, studied policy wordings, completed medical questionnaires, submitted medical records and went through actual underwriting with several insurers.

The experience taught me one important lesson:

Choosing health insurance in Thailand is not about finding the highest advertised coverage. It is about understanding what the insurer will actually cover for you after underwriting.

A policy may advertise THB 10 million, THB 30 million or even THB 100 million of medical coverage. But that number means surprisingly little until you know the exclusions, premium loadings, occupational restrictions and renewal conditions that apply to you personally.

Why This Matters for Dive Professionals

Working as a dive professional in Thailand is very different from having an office job.

A dive professional may spend hundreds of hours underwater every year while also operating or working around boats, handling heavy scuba equipment, travelling by road, working in wet and physically demanding environments and accepting a certain level of occupational risk.

In my own case, I make approximately 420 dives per year, with an average underwater time of around 50 minutes per dive and regular depths of up to approximately 32 metres.

That works out at roughly 350 hours underwater per year.

This turned out to matter considerably during insurance underwriting.

Some insurers were prepared to assess the risk individually. Others excluded certain diving-related risks. Some personal accident products would not accept professional divers at all, and one health insurance product could not accept an applicant whose occupation included professional scuba diving.

You will not discover these things simply by comparing premiums on an insurance website.

My Situation

I am 46 years old, have lived in Thailand since 2011 and own Chang Diving Center on Koh Chang.

I am a professional diving instructor and instructor trainer.

Like many people in their forties, I also have controlled hypertension and treated elevated cholesterol.

During the application process I provided insurers with extensive medical documentation, including blood tests, medical reports and imaging.

My approach was simple:

Tell the insurer everything before buying the policy.

I would rather receive an exclusion, premium loading or even a rejection before paying for insurance than discover during a major claim that the insurer considers something undeclared.

The Most Important Lesson: Get Underwritten Before Comparing

An insurance quotation and an underwriting offer are not the same thing.

A quotation tells you what the policy costs for a standard applicant.

Underwriting tells you what the policy actually costs for you and what it actually covers for you.

The difference can be enormous.

One insurer may initially look cheaper but add a 25% or 50% risk loading after reviewing your medical history.

Another may accept you at a reasonable premium but exclude certain medical conditions and their complications.

Another may offer excellent medical coverage but reject the application because professional scuba diving is part of your occupation.

My preferred process therefore became:

Quotation → Application → Full Medical Disclosure → Underwriting → Written Acceptance Terms → Comparison → Decision → Payment

Not:

Quotation → Cheapest Premium → Buy

What Pacific Cross Taught Me About Diving Exclusions

One of the most useful clarifications during this process came from Pacific Cross.

Their health insurance exclusion refers to diving-related disorders and illnesses rather than simply excluding me because I work as a diving instructor.

After requesting clarification, diving-related medical conditions were explained as including conditions such as:

  • decompression sickness (DCS),
  • barotrauma,
  • arterial gas embolism (AGE),
  • nitrogen narcosis,
  • oxygen toxicity,
  • and secondary complications resulting from these conditions.

The important distinction is causation.

A decompression accident after a dive would fall within the diving exclusion.

An unrelated medical condition would not automatically become a diving-related condition simply because I work as a diving instructor.

This is particularly important for me because I maintain specialist diving accident insurance separately through DAN.

Health Insurance Accepted – Personal Accident Insurance Rejected

Pacific Cross also demonstrated something else that dive professionals should understand:

The company was prepared to offer me health insurance, but its Personal Accident insurance was not available to me because of my scuba diving occupation.

In other words, even products from the same insurance group can assess your occupation very differently.

LUMA: When the Occupation Itself Becomes the Problem

LUMA was a useful example of a completely different underwriting issue.

The problem was not simply whether decompression sickness or another diving accident would be excluded.

The product excluded practising sport professionally, and after clarification I was informed that the application could not be accepted under this product if scuba diving or diving instruction was my occupation.

That immediately ended the application for me.

This taught me that every professional diver should ask two separate questions:

Is scuba diving covered?
and
Will you accept me if professional scuba diving or diving instruction is my occupation?

Those are not the same question.

Krungthai-AXA: Accepted – But at What Cost?

Krungthai-AXA demonstrated another reason why underwriting needs to happen before comparing prices.

My application was accepted, but the proposed underwriting conditions included a 50% risk-based premium surcharge because of BMI.

The proposed exclusions also included hypertension, coronary artery disease and cerebrovascular disease caused by high blood pressure, as well as high blood lipid levels.

One positive point was that I was informed that the high blood lipid exclusion could potentially be reviewed and removed after maintaining normal lipid levels for three years.

Nevertheless, paying 150% of the standard premium while simultaneously accepting significant medical exclusions did not make financial sense to me at this stage.

This does not mean Krungthai-AXA is a bad insurer.

It simply means that its underwriting decision did not suit my individual situation at this time.

Losing additional weight and applying again in the future may produce a very different result.

Allianz Ayudhya: Underwriting in Progress

Allianz Ayudhya is currently one of my remaining candidates.

The insurer has received extensive medical documentation and subsequently requested additional information, including permission to obtain parts of my medical history directly from the hospital.

I actually consider this a positive part of the process.

The objective should be to disclose and clarify everything before the policy is issued.

The proposal I am currently evaluating offers substantial inpatient coverage and, importantly, ordinary accident treatment is already integrated into the health insurance benefits.

This means that a separate Personal Accident policy may not necessarily be required for medical accident expenses, depending on the final underwriting terms.

APRIL MyHEALTH Thailand / Sompo

APRIL International proposed its MyHEALTH Thailand Essential plan.

The current quotation I am evaluating is approximately:

  • THB 32,750 annual deductible
  • approximately THB 61,233 annual premium before final underwriting

The product is underwritten by Sompo Insurance (Thailand) PCL, while APRIL arranges and administers the policy.

The Essential plan includes substantial hospital and surgery benefits. Major hospital expenses such as single-room accommodation, ICU, theatre fees, general hospital costs, surgical implants, diagnostic scans and professional fees are generally covered within the applicable policy limits and conditions.

Cancer treatment is also an important part of the cover, including active inpatient and outpatient cancer treatment subject to the policy terms.

Interestingly, the Essential plan also includes limited routine outpatient benefits without requiring a full OPD module, together with emergency-room treatment for qualifying emergencies.

Professional diving remains one of the key underwriting questions.

APRIL / Sompo requested detailed information about my actual diving exposure, including:

  • around 50 minutes underwater per dive,

  • and no diving-related hospitalisation.

The standard policy wording contains restrictions relating to diving depth and certain higher-risk activities, so the final underwriting decision will be particularly important.

Health Insurance vs Personal Accident Insurance

This is another area where my opinion changed during the research.

Originally, my strategy was:

Health Insurance + Personal Accident Insurance + DAN

I no longer believe a separate Personal Accident policy is automatically necessary.

A good health insurance policy may already cover medical treatment resulting from ordinary accidents.

A separate PA policy may still provide additional benefits such as:

  • accidental death benefit,
  • permanent disability benefit,
  • additional accident medical benefits,
  • daily hospital income.

Those benefits can be useful, but the first question should always be:

What does my health insurance already cover after an accident?

Otherwise you may simply be paying for overlapping insurance.

For professional divers there is another complication: some PA products may reject the occupation even when a health insurer is prepared to accept it.

DAN Diving Accident Insurance

One part of my insurance strategy has not changed.

As a professional dive instructor, I believe every diver – and especially every dive professional – should consider dedicated diving accident insurance.

Standard health insurance may exclude diving completely, impose depth limits, exclude professional diving or exclude specific diving-related illnesses.

DAN – Divers Alert Network – exists specifically for diving-related medical emergencies.

Typical diving-related problems can include:

  • decompression sickness (DCS),
  • arterial gas embolism (AGE),
  • barotrauma,
  • hyperbaric chamber treatment,
  • diving-related evacuation and emergency assistance.

This makes specialist diving insurance fundamentally different from ordinary health insurance.

Why Full OPD Cover May Not Be Necessary

My thinking about outpatient cover has also changed during this process.

OPD does not simply mean “accident treatment”.

Some health insurance policies already include emergency accident treatment or emergency-room benefits within the main medical plan.

OPD is primarily useful for ordinary outpatient healthcare such as:

  • doctor consultations,
  • medication,
  • diagnostic tests,
  • specialist consultations,
  • physiotherapy,
  • routine treatment where no hospital admission is required.

In Thailand, many smaller outpatient expenses are relatively affordable.

I therefore compare the additional OPD premium with the amount I would realistically expect to spend myself during a normal year.

For me, insurance should primarily protect against medical expenses that could seriously damage my financial future.

Small everyday medical bills are much easier to self-insure.

The Importance of a Good Insurance Broker

Another major lesson from this process is that the broker can be almost as important as the insurer.

A good broker does not simply send a quotation.

A good broker helps clarify:

  • policy wording,
  • medical exclusions,
  • occupational exclusions,
  • deductibles,
  • premium development,
  • claim procedures,
  • renewal conditions,
  • and practical examples of how exclusions may work in real life.

During my own application process, repeated questions to insurers produced important clarifications that were not obvious from the original quotation.

This is particularly important for dive professionals because terms such as diving-related, professional sport, occupational risk, causes and complications or pre-existing condition can completely change the practical value of a policy.

Questions Every Dive Professional Should Ask Before Buying Health Insurance

  1. Will you accept professional scuba diving or diving instruction as my occupation?
  2. Is recreational scuba diving covered?
  3. Is professional diving covered?
  4. Is there a maximum diving depth?
  5. Are decompression sickness, AGE and barotrauma excluded?
  6. Does a diving exclusion apply only to injuries caused by diving, or can it also apply to ordinary accidents occurring during diving work?
  7. Are ordinary accidents already covered by the health insurance?
  8. What pre-existing conditions will be excluded after underwriting?
  9. Does an exclusion also include causes, complications or related conditions?
  10. Can exclusions be reviewed or removed in the future?
  11. Will a medical or occupational premium loading be applied?
  12. Can that loading later be reduced or removed?
  13. Is the deductible annual, per disability, per admission or per claim?
  14. Can co-payment be introduced at renewal?
  15. Can the insurer refuse renewal because of claims?
  16. Can an individual premium be increased because of future claims?
  17. Who is the actual legal insurer or risk carrier?
  18. Which country's law governs the policy?
  19. Is pre-authorisation required before planned hospitalisation or surgery?
Get important answers in writing.

The Importance of Full Medical Disclosure

One principle became increasingly important during this process:

Disclose first. Discuss the underwriting decision afterwards.

I provided insurers with extensive medical documentation and, where requested, authorised insurers to obtain additional hospital records directly.

That may result in exclusions or premium loadings.

But I prefer knowing those conditions before I accept the policy.

The worst health insurance is not necessarily a policy with clearly defined exclusions.

A much worse situation is believing you are fully insured for years and only discovering during a major claim that the insurer disputes information from the original application.

Thailand's New Co-Payment Rules

Another subject worth understanding is co-payment at renewal.

Newer Thai health insurance policies may contain provisions allowing a co-payment to be introduced in a future insurance year under conditions permitted by Thailand's insurance regulator.

This does not mean every insured person automatically pays 30% of every hospital bill.

The important question is whether your policy allows a future co-payment to be triggered following certain claim patterns and what conditions apply.

This is therefore another renewal condition that should be understood before choosing long-term health insurance.

Final Thoughts – For Now

After months of research, my biggest lesson is surprisingly simple:

Don't choose health insurance from the brochure.

The brochure tells you what the product can cover.

Underwriting tells you what your policy will cover.

For dive professionals, this distinction is particularly important because the occupation itself can change the insurer's decision.

One company may accept you but exclude diving-related illnesses.

Another may impose a maximum diving depth.

Another may provide health insurance but refuse Personal Accident insurance.

And another may refuse the entire health product because professional diving is your occupation.

There is therefore no universal “best health insurance for dive professionals in Thailand”.

The right policy is the one that:

  • accepts your real occupation,
  • clearly defines its exclusions,
  • protects the major financial risks you actually care about,
  • has a deductible you can comfortably afford,
  • remains reasonably affordable as you get older,
  • and gives you confidence that you understand what will happen when you make a claim.
